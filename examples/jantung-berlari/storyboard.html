f1A normal resting heart rate for adults is 60 to 100 beats a minute (well-trained people can be lower). The film uses 75 beats a minute as its resting example, the textbook's worked value.American Heart Association, 'Target Heart Rates Chart' (heart.org/en/healthy-living/fitness/fitness-basics/target-heart-rates): resting heart rate 60-100 bpm; OpenStax, Anatomy & Physiology 2e, 19.4 'Cardiac Physiology' (normal resting HR 60-100 bpm).
f2Cardiac output (blood pumped by a ventricle each minute) = heart rate x stroke volume. At rest a stroke volume of about 70 mL is typical: 75 x 70 mL = 5250 mL = 5,25 L a minute (normal range 4 to 8 L a minute). The film says 'sekitar tujuh puluh lima', 'kurang lebih tujuh puluh mililiter' and 'kira-kira lima liter', and shows '5,25 L' as five and a quarter one-litre bottles.OpenStax, Anatomy & Physiology 2e (Betts et al.), 19.4 'Cardiac Physiology': CO = HR x SV, worked example 75 bpm x 70 mL = 5.25 L/min, resting CO 4.0-8.0 L/min (same text in Indiana University 'Human Physiology' Pressbooks, 'Cardiac output').
f3In healthy young people the heart rate may rise to 150 beats a minute or more during exercise, and the stroke volume from about 70 to about 130 mL through stronger contraction: 150 x 130 mL = 19500 mL = 19,5 L a minute. 19,5 / 5,25 = 3.71, which the film says as 'nyaris empat kali lipat' and shows as '≈ 3,7×' (the textbook itself rounds this to '4-5 times the resting rate'; the film keeps the lower, computed figure). Real values vary with age, size, sex and fitness; trained endurance athletes reach higher outputs.OpenStax, Anatomy & Physiology 2e, 19.4 'Cardiac Physiology': 'In healthy young individuals, HR may increase to 150 bpm or higher during exercise. SV can also increase from 70 to approximately 130 mL due to increased strength of contraction. This would increase CO to approximately 19.5 L/min'. f4During physical activity the muscles use more oxygen than at rest, and aerobic activity makes the heart beat faster than usual. The film shows no number for the muscle's oxygen use, only 'jauh lebih banyak' and a low-oxygen warning.NIH National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, 'Physical Activity and Your Heart' (nhlbi.nih.gov/health/heart/physical-activity).
f5Red blood cells pick up oxygen in the lungs and carry it in the blood to the body's tissues; the left side of the heart pumps this oxygen-rich blood out through the aorta to the body. Simplification: the film shows one artery from the heart to the thighs and one O2 pixel per cell; it does not show the lungs.NHLBI, 'How the Heart Works' (nhlbi.nih.gov/health/heart/blood-flow); U.S. National Library of Medicine, 'Circulation Station' (nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/changing-the-face-of-medicine/resources/circulation-station.html).
f6In exercise, receptors in muscles, joints and tendons (proprioreceptors) fire more often; the cardiovascular centres in the brainstem respond through the sympathetic cardioaccelerator nerves, which release norepinephrine at the SA node (the heart's natural pacemaker, in the right atrium) and the heart muscle, raising the heart rate and the strength of contraction (and so the stroke volume). Simplification: the film says 'otot ngirim sinyal ke otak, otak nyuruh jantung ngegas lewat saraf'; it leaves out the brain's own command at the start of exercise, adrenaline from the adrenal glands, chemoreceptors, and the extra blood returning to the heart (Frank-Starling).OpenStax, Anatomy & Physiology 2e, 19.4 'Cardiac Physiology', 'Autonomic Innervation of the Heart' and its table of factors increasing HR and force of contraction (proprioreceptors: 'increased rates of firing during exercise'), read through search results quoting the Lumen Learning copies; BYU-Idaho BIO 265 text, 'Extrinsic regulation' (sympathetic fibres steepen the pacemaker potential).
f7Maximum heart rate is estimated as about 220 minus age; vigorous activity is about 70-85 % of it. For a 20-year-old: 220 - 20 = 200, and 150 is 75% of it, so the film's 150 is an ordinary hard run, not a maximum. (The formula is an estimate; individuals can differ by about 15 bpm.)American Heart Association, 'Target Heart Rates Chart' (as f1); Harvard Health Publishing, 'All about your heart rate' (on the formula's spread).
