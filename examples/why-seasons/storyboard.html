Why do we have seasons?Why do we have seasons?

PromiseSeasons come from the tilt of the Earth's axis, not from its distance to the Sun: the half of the Earth leaning toward the Sun gets steeper, more concentrated sunlight and longer days, and that is its summer.
AudienceCurious viewers from about twelve up, anywhere in the world. They know the Earth goes round the Sun once a year; many believe summer is when the Earth is nearer the Sun.
Length43.953 s · 14 shots · 38 drawn frames · 1920×1080 · 60 fps · en · voice: Megan
Look#ffb43a #5fb0ff
A dark graphite stage. Amber is sunlight and summer, cool blue is winter, and they keep those meanings all film. The stage's light sits where the Sun is: left of the January Earth, right of the June Earth, high for the steep beam, low and blue for the December one. The Earth is the real globe from NASA's maps; the diagrams are thin glowing lines; panels are glass, headings are metal.

IdClaimSource
f1The Earth's axis is tilted 23.44 degrees from the upright of its orbit (its obliquity); the film says and shows 23.4.NASA Goddard, Earth Fact Sheet (obliquity to orbit 23.44 deg); IAU value 23.4393 deg for epoch J2000
f2The Earth is closest to the Sun (perihelion, 147.1 million km) in early January, about 3 January, and farthest (aphelion, 152.1 million km) in early July, about 4 July. The difference is 5.0 million km: 5.0 / 152.1 = 3.3%.NASA Goddard, Earth Fact Sheet (perihelion 147.095, aphelion 152.100 million km); NASA Science, Milankovitch (Orbital) Cycles (perihelion on or about 3 January, aphelion about 4 July)
f3The Earth's orbit is nearly a circle: its eccentricity is 0.0167, so the Sun sits 1.67% of the orbit's half-width off its centre. The film's orbit is a circle seen at an angle, with the Sun offset by that amount; (1 - 0.0167) / (1 + 0.0167) = 0.967 = 147.1 / 152.1.NASA Goddard, Earth Fact Sheet (orbit eccentricity 0.0167)
f4The axis keeps pointing in the same direction in space all year (toward Polaris), so the northern half leans toward the Sun around the June solstice (20-21 June) and away from it around the December solstice (21-22 December). The slow 26,000-year drift of the axis is left out.NASA Space Place, What Causes the Seasons?; NOAA SciJinks, What Causes the Seasons?
f5Seasons are opposite in the two hemispheres: June to August is summer in the north and winter in the south, December to February the reverse. In early January the north is in winter.NASA Space Place, What Causes the Seasons?; NOAA National Weather Service, The Seasons, the Equinox and the Solstices
f6At 40 degrees north the noon Sun stands 90 - 40 + 23.44 = 73.44 degrees above the horizon at the June solstice and 90 - 40 - 23.44 = 26.56 degrees at the December solstice.Arithmetic from f1 (noon height = 90 - latitude + the Sun's declination); NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory, Solar Calculator
f7A beam of sunlight of a given width covers ground in proportion to 1 / sin(the Sun's height): 1 / sin 73.44 = 1.043 widths in June, 1 / sin 26.56 = 2.236 widths in December, so 2.236 / 1.043 = 2.14 times the ground (said as 'twice', shown as 2.1x). The light on each square metre falls in the same ratio. This is at noon at 40 N, ignoring the extra air the low beam crosses.Lambert's cosine law; arithmetic from f6; NASA Earth Observatory, Climate and Earth's Energy Budget (sunlight at a low angle is spread over more surface)
f8At 40 degrees north the longest day has 15 h 02 min of daylight and the shortest 9 h 20 min (said as 'fifteen hours against nine'). The drawn paths use the Sun's height = asin(sin 40 sin d + cos 40 cos d cos(15 (t - 12))) with d = +23.44 and -23.44 degrees.U.S. Naval Observatory, Astronomical Applications: Comparative Lengths of Days and Nights (40 N: 15h02m, 9h20m)
