f1Van Helmont's willow experiment (published 1648, so 'abad ke-17' on screen): 200 pounds of oven-dried earth in an earthen pot, a willow stem of 5 pounds, watered only with rainwater or distilled water; after five years the tree weighed 169 pounds and about 3 ounces, 'therefore 164 pounds of Wood, Barks, and Roots', while the dried earth was 'the same two hundred pounds, wanting about two ounces'. At the avoirdupois pound (0.45359 kg): soil 200 lb = 90.7 kg (screen: 91 kg), the stem 5 lb = 2.27 kg (screen: 2,3 kg), the gain 164 lb = 74.4 kg (screen: +74 kg; voice: 'tujuh puluh kilo lebih'), the soil's loss 2 oz = 57 g (screen: −57 g; voice: 'sekitar setengah ons', f10). 56.7 g / 74389 g = 0.076 %, under 0.1 % (screen: '< 0,1 %'). The bars in s05 are 760 px and 0.58 px. Caveats kept off screen: the pound Helmont used may differ from the avoirdupois pound by some per cent (the rounded figures hold either way); he weighed the tree fresh but the soil dry; and his weighing could not have been very accurate (Hershey). The film uses it only for what it shows: very little of the tree's weight came from the soil.J. B. van Helmont, Ortus Medicinae (1648), English translation 1662, as quoted in D. R. Hershey, 'Misconceptions about Helmont's Willow Experiment', Plant Science Bulletin 49(3): 78-83 (2003), Botanical Society of America (botany.org, PSB_2003_49_3.pdf), including the caveats on fresh vs dry weight and accuracy.
f2A tree does not eat the soil: the dry matter of a plant is on average about 45 % carbon, 45 % oxygen and 6 % hydrogen (96 % together), and the carbon, taken from the CO₂ in the air, makes up the majority of the dry mass; mineral nutrients from the soil are the few per cent that remain. So a growing tree takes very little from the soil itself ('tanah di bawahnya hampir nggak berkurang'). Simplification: the film does not mention the minerals; the water does come up through the soil (f5).OpenStax, Biology 2e, 31.1 'Nutritional Requirements of Plants' (openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/31-1-nutritional-requirements-of-plants): 'Carbon that was obtained from atmospheric CO2 ... composes the majority of the dry mass within most plants'; 'the dry weight ... of a cell is 45 percent carbon ... oxygen (45 percent) and hydrogen (6 percent)'.
f3Photosynthesis uses sunlight, carbon dioxide and water to make sugar, and gives off oxygen: 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂, with light as the energy source. Plants make their own food this way (photoautotrophs, 'self-feeders using light'). The equation is on screen in s15; the voice says 'bikin makanannya sendiri, dari cahaya, udara, dan air'.OpenStax, Biology 2e, 8.1 'Overview of Photosynthesis', Figure 8.5 (the equation: 'six carbon dioxide and six water molecules produce one sugar molecule and six oxygen molecules ... Sunlight is used as an energy source') and the text on photoautotrophs (openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/8-1-overview-of-photosynthesis).
f4Carbon dioxide enters the leaf (and oxygen leaves it) through small, regulated openings called stomata, which are typically on the underside of the leaf. The voice calls them 'pori-pori kecil di daun'; the tag reads 'pori daun (stomata)'. The pore in s07 is drawn much larger than life.OpenStax, Biology 2e, 8.1: 'The gas exchange of carbon dioxide and oxygen occurs through small, regulated openings called stomata ... typically located on the underside of the leaf'.
f5Plant roots absorb water from the soil through root hairs and transport it up to the leaves through the xylem (the cream channel in s08), pulled up mainly by transpiration.OpenStax, Biology 2e, 31.1 ('Plant roots absorb water from the soil through root hairs and transport it up to the leaves through the xylem') and 30.5 'Transport of Water and Solutes in Plants'.
f6Inside the leaf's cells, chloroplasts hold stacks of thylakoids (grana); chlorophyll, embedded in the thylakoid membranes, is the pigment that absorbs the light. Chlorophyll absorbs blue and red light but not green, which it reflects, so it looks green ('zat hijau').OpenStax, Biology 2e, 8.1 ('Embedded in the thylakoid membrane is chlorophyll, a pigment (molecule that absorbs light)') and 8.2 'The Light-Dependent Reactions of Photosynthesis' ('Chlorophyll a absorbs wavelengths from either end of the visible spectrum (blue and red), but not green ... chlorophyll appears green').
f7Light energy drives the splitting of water in the light-dependent reactions: splitting one H₂O releases two electrons, two hydrogen ions and one oxygen atom; two split waters make one O₂. The oxygen given off by photosynthesis comes from this water; most of it escapes to the atmosphere, where aerobic organisms (us) use it to breathe. s10 shows one molecule split; s11 shows O₂ (pairs) reaching a person.OpenStax, Biology 2e, 8.2: 'water is "split" during this stage of photosynthesis ... Splitting one H2O molecule releases two electrons, two hydrogen atoms, and one atom of oxygen ... splitting two molecules is required to form one molecule of diatomic O2 gas ... The remainder escapes to the atmosphere where it is used by aerobic organisms to support respiration'.
f8The hydrogen and the energy from the light reactions (carried as NADPH and ATP) are used in the Calvin cycle to turn carbon dioxide into sugar (G3P; two G3P make a glucose, which takes six turns of the cycle, one CO₂ each). Simplification, on screen and in the voice: 'Hidrogennya digabung sama karbon dioksida, jadi gula'; NADPH, ATP and G3P are not named, the hexagon stands for the sugar molecule (glucose's ring), and s12 shows fewer pieces than one glucose needs.OpenStax, Biology 2e, 8.3 'Using Light Energy to Make Organic Molecules' (openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/8-3-using-light-energy-to-make-organic-molecules): NADPH loses 'both energy and a hydrogen atom' in making G3P from CO2; 'six times to produce a six-carbon glucose molecule'; and 8.1 ('Glucose ... is made from two three-carbon G3Ps').
f9The sugars made by photosynthesis are the plant's food (its source of energy) and its building material: they are carried from the leaves to the growing parts through the phloem, and glucose chains make cellulose, the main part of plant cell walls (over thirty per cent of plant matter); wood is mostly cellulose. s14 draws cellulose as chains of the sugar hexagon. Simplification: wood also holds hemicellulose and lignin, which are made from the same sugars but are not shown.OpenStax, Biology 2e, 30.5 ('The products of photosynthesis are called photosynthates ... Sugars produced in sources, such as leaves, need to be delivered to growing parts of the plant via the phloem'); 3.2 'Carbohydrates' ('Wood and paper are mostly cellulosic in nature. Glucose monomers comprise cellulose'); 31.1 (cellulose 'makes up over thirty percent of plant matter').
f10In Indonesian everyday use one ons is one hundred grams, so the soil's loss of about 57 g is said as 'sekitar setengah ons' (half an ons is 50 g; the screen shows the exact −57 g).Kamus Besar Bahasa Indonesia, entry 'ons': 'satuan ukuran berat (massa) seratus gram' (read at kbbi.web.id/ons; kbbi.kemdikbud.go.id did not answer).
