Why does a² + b² = c² always work?Everyone learns a² + b² = c², but why is it true for every right triangle?

Promisea² is the area of a square on side a; four identical right triangles in a square of side a + b leave c² empty, and moved around they leave a² and b² empty, so c² = a² + b² for any right triangle.
AudienceCurious teenagers and adults anywhere, watching on a phone or a laptop, often with the sound off at first. They remember a² + b² = c² from school and can use it, but have never seen why it is true, and think of 'squared' as just a sum to do.

IdClaimSource
f1In any right triangle the square on the side opposite the right angle (the hypotenuse c) equals the sum of the squares on the two other sides (the legs a and b): a² + b² = c². The film says it for 'any right triangle you draw' and writes 'a² + b² = c²'.Euclid, Elements I.47, in D. E. Joyce's edition, Clark University (mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/propI47.html): 'In right-angled triangles the square on the side opposite the right angle equals the sum of the squares on the sides containing the right angle.' OpenStax Prealgebra 2e, §9.3 (openstax.org/books/prealgebra-2e/pages/9-3-use-properties-of-angles-triangles-and-the-pythagorean-theorem): 'In any right triangle, a² + b² = c², where c is the length of the hypotenuse and a and b are the lengths of the legs.'
f2a² is the area of a square with side a: a square is a rectangle with length = width = a, and a rectangle's area is length × width, which counts the unit squares that cover it. Euclid's own statement of the theorem is about these squares ('the square on the side'), not about a number squared.OpenStax Prealgebra 2e, §9.4 'Use Properties of Rectangles, Triangles, and Trapezoids' (area as the number of unit squares that cover a region; the rug 2 ft by 3 ft is 6 squares; area of a rectangle = L × W); Euclid I.47 as f1.
f3The triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5 is a right triangle: 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5² (by the converse of the theorem, Euclid I.48, the angle between the sides 3 and 4 is right). The square on 3 holds 3 × 3 = 9 unit squares, the one on 4 holds 4 × 4 = 16, the one on 5 holds 5 × 5 = 25. The same 3-4-5 case is drawn in the ancient Chinese 'hypotenuse diagram' of the Zhou bi suan jing (not shown in the film).Arithmetic; Euclid I.48 (Joyce, Clark University, mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/propI48.html); Joyce's note to I.47 on Plimpton 322 and the Zhou bi hypotenuse diagram ('the smallest of these is 3, 4, 5').
f4The rearrangement proof. A square of side a + b holds four copies of the right triangle (legs a, b). Placed at the four corners, they leave a tilted square of side c in the middle: area (a + b)² = 4 triangles + c². Moved (two of them slide, none turns or changes), they leave a square a × a and a square b × b: area (a + b)² = 4 triangles + a² + b². The big square and the four triangles are the same both times, so c² = a² + b². In the film's drawing a = 240 px and b = 320 px (the 3 : 4 triangle); triangle 3 slides by (−a, −b) and triangle 2 by (−b, +a), and the holes left are 240 × 240 and 320 × 320 (checked in storyboard.build.py: 4 × ½ab + a² + b² = (a + b)²).NRICH (University of Cambridge, Millennium Mathematics Project), 'Pythagoras Proofs', problem 6553, method 2 and its published solution (nrich.maths.org/problems/pythagoras-proofs): 'The area of the first square is given by (a+b)² or 4(½ab) + c². The area of the second square is given by (a+b)² or 4(½ab) + a² + b² ... Subtracting equals from both sides we have a² + b² = c².' Triangle area ½bh: OpenStax Prealgebra 2e §9.4.
f5The middle shape is a true square: its four sides are each a hypotenuse c, and at each of its corners the two triangles' acute angles (x and y) and the square's corner make a straight line, 180°. The acute angles of a right triangle add up to 90° (the three angles of a triangle add up to 180°, one is 90°), so the corner is 180° − 90° = 90°. On screen: 'x + y = 90°' and 'so this corner is 90° too'.OpenStax Prealgebra 2e §9.3: 'For any ΔABC, the sum of the measures of the angles is 180°' and 'A right triangle has one 90° angle'. NRICH 'Pythagoras Proofs' solution, which points out this step (Lewis's diagram, 'x + y = 90°') as the piece a proof by this picture must not leave out.
f6It holds only for right triangles: when the angle between a and b is obtuse, the square on the third side is larger than a² + b²; when it is acute, smaller (Euclid's geometric form of the law of cosines). The film says 'any right triangle', rings the right angle, and writes 'right triangles only' in the recap; the proof needs the right angle in f5.Euclid, Elements II.12 and II.13 (Joyce, Clark University, mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookII/propII12.html and propII13.html); Joyce's note to I.47: 'Propositions II.12 and II.13 consider triangles other than right triangles ... geometric forms of the law of cosines.'
f7The proof uses nothing but the legs a and b of one right triangle and four copies of it, so it works for every right triangle, flat or tall. The film's two other triangles are drawn with legs 64 and 296 px (flat) and 250 and 110 px (tall); their pairs follow the same two moves.NRICH 'Pythagoras Proofs' (as f4): the proof is written for general a, b, c; the mini figures are computed in storyboard.build.py.
