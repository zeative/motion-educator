How does a neural network learn?Nobody writes the rules for the AI you use every day. So how does it learn?

PromiseA neural network learns by guessing, measuring how wrong the guess is (the error), and nudging every weight a little in the direction that makes the error smaller, over and over; nobody writes its rules.
AudienceCurious teens and adults anywhere who use AI every day (chatbots, photo filters, recommendations) but have never seen inside one. They have heard that 'AI is trained', but not what actually changes when it learns.

Fact sheet

IdClaimSource
n1The AI people use is machine learning: instead of programmers writing the rules for the task, a model is trained on data until it has learned the relationship itself. Simplification: engineers still write the training program, choose the data and check the result; what nobody writes is the rules for the task ('if it meows → cat').Google, Machine Learning: Introduction to ML, 'What is ML?' (developers.google.com/machine-learning/intro-to-ml/what-is-ml): 'ML is the process of training a piece of software, called a model, to make useful predictions or generate content ... from data', contrasted there with the traditional approach of writing the model by hand.
n2A neural network is nodes (neurons) in layers, joined by connections; each neuron takes the sum of its inputs times a weight per connection, plus a bias, and passes it through an activation function. A weight is how strongly a connection passes its input on. In s05 the two lines are two of the toy network's starting weights, 0.92 and 0.28 (their sizes; the sign is not shown).Google Machine Learning Crash Course, 'Neural networks: Nodes and hidden layers' (developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/neural-networks/nodes-hidden-layers): 'take the sum of the product of each of its inputs ... and a unique weight parameter, plus the bias'.
n3The film's network is a real toy, trained in storyboard.build.py: three yes/no inputs (meow?, bark?, four legs?: 1 = yes, 0 = no), three hidden neurons and one output, sigmoid activations, 12 weights (the lines drawn) plus 4 biases (not drawn). Three examples: cat (1, 0, 1) → 1, dog (0, 1, 1) → 0, fish (0, 0, 0) → 0. Starting weights and biases random between −1 and 1 (seeded, seed 8), as training usually starts. Line widths are drawn from the sizes of the weights. Simplification: real image networks read millions of pixel values, not three yes/no answers; this one is small enough to watch.Computed (storyboard.build.py). Method: Google ML Crash Course, 'Linear regression: Gradient descent' ('The model begins training with randomized weights and biases near zero'); Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (1986), 'Learning representations by back-propagating errors', Nature 323, 533–536.
n4The first guess for the cat is 0.269 → shown as 0.27; the right answer is 1, so the gap (the error) is 1 − 0.27 = 0.73. The film calls the gap 'error'; training makes the squared gap small (squared error), which is the same aim. 'Loss is a numerical value indicating the difference between a model's predictions and the actual values.'Computed (storyboard.build.py); Google ML Crash Course, 'Linear regression: Loss' (developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/linear-regression/loss).
n5Learning is gradient descent: work out, for every weight, which way to move it to make the error smaller, move it a small amount that way, and repeat. Backpropagation is how a network works out that direction for every weight: the error is sent backwards from the output through the connections. 'The procedure repeatedly adjusts the weights of the connections in the network so as to minimize a measure of the difference between the actual output vector of the net and the desired output vector.'Google ML Crash Course, 'Linear regression: Gradient descent' (steps: calculate the loss, determine the direction that reduces it, move the weights and bias a small amount, repeat) and 'Neural networks: Training using backpropagation' ('Backpropagation is the most common training algorithm for neural networks'); Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (1986), Nature 323, 533–536, abstract.
n6One nudge (one gradient step on the cat, step size 2.0): the weight from the middle hidden neuron to the output goes 0.661 → 0.791 (shown 0.66 → 0.79), every other weight touched by the error moves too, and the guess for the same cat goes 0.27 → 0.40: the error 0.73 → 0.60. The step size (learning rate) is set large in this toy so one step is visible; real training takes much smaller steps.Computed (storyboard.build.py); learning rate: Google ML Crash Course, 'Linear regression: Hyperparameters' ('The learning rate determines the magnitude of the changes to make to the weights and bias during each step').
n7Repeated over the three examples for 1,000 rounds (one nudge per example per round), the network's answers become cat 0.983, dog 0.009, fish 0.015 (shown 0.98, 0.01, 0.01). The mean squared error over the three falls from 0.227 to 0.00019; the chart in s15 shows its first 60 rounds, after which it lies flat (it is below 0.01 by round 50). A loss curve that falls and flattens is what training looks like. As built, the chart in s15 plots the cat's own error (1 − its guess, the gap the meter shows): 0.73 at the start, 0.60 after the one nudge, 0.64 after round 1, 0.10 at round 60, and 0.02 after round 1,000 (written at the right end of the chart), so its numbers continue the meter's; the mean squared error above is the measure training itself makes small.Computed (storyboard.build.py); Google ML Crash Course, 'Linear regression: Gradient descent' (loss curves; 'A model is considered to have converged when further iterations do not significantly reduce the loss').
n8Nobody set which input matters. At the start the lines from 'bark?' were the strongest (sum of the three weights' sizes: meow 1.95, bark 2.34, four legs 1.54); after training the lines from 'meow?' are (meow 7.10, bark 4.53, four legs 3.47). In the three examples, meowing is what tells a cat apart. This is the toy's result; in big networks what each weight 'means' is usually not readable like this.Computed (storyboard.build.py); Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (1986): as a result of the weight adjustments, hidden units 'come to represent important features of the task domain'.
n9Big models learn the same way, with far more weights: GPT-3 (2020) has 175 billion parameters (on screen '175,000,000,000' and '175 billion weights'), trained with Adam (a form of gradient descent) on 300 billion tokens; its 'error' is how wrong its guess of the next word is. Against the toy's 12 weights the drawing cannot be to scale and says so; the zoom-out passes a field of a million weights on the way, a scale mark between the two and not any particular model. Simplifications: the film calls parameters 'weights' (they also include biases and embeddings), and Adam adjusts each step's size, but each step still moves the weights to reduce the error.Brown et al. (2020), 'Language Models are Few-Shot Learners', arXiv:2005.14165: 'we train GPT-3, an autoregressive language model with 175 billion parameters'; §2.3 and Appendix B: 'All models were trained for a total of 300 billion tokens'; 'To train all versions of GPT-3, we use Adam'.
