How does your phone know where you are?How does your phone know where you are?

PromiseA GPS receiver only listens: each satellite broadcasts the time, the delay of each signal times the speed of light gives a distance, each distance puts you on a sphere around that satellite, and four satellites pin down your position and correct the receiver's own clock.
AudienceCurious adults and teenagers anywhere, watching on a phone. They use maps every day and may think the phone sends something to the satellites; they know light is fast but not how a distance comes from a time.

Fact sheet

IdClaimSource
f1GPS satellites fly in medium Earth orbit at an altitude of about 20,200 km (12,550 miles), circling the Earth twice a day. From the Earth's centre that is 20,200 + 6,371 = 26,571 km, 4.17 Earth radii: the film draws the orbits at that ratio.GPS.gov, Space Segment (gps.gov/systems/gps/space), read through search summaries (the page could not be opened from this session); ESA Navipedia, GPS Space Segment. Mean Earth radius 6,371 km (IUGG).
f2A signal from a satellite straight overhead travels 20,200 km: 20,200 / 299,792 km/s = 0.0674 s, about 67 milliseconds, written as 'about a fifteenth of a second' (1/15 = 0.0667 s). From satellites lower in the sky it takes longer (up to about 86 ms at the horizon).Arithmetic from f1 and the speed of light (BIPM SI Brochure, 9th ed.: c = 299,792,458 m/s exactly). The same order is given by the USDA Forest Service 'How GPS works' note (about 0.06 s) and Science News Today (about 67 ms for 20,000 km). The signal is slowed slightly in the atmosphere; ignored here.
f3Light travels 299,792,458 m/s, so in one microsecond (a millionth of a second) it covers 299.8 m, about 300 m; an error of 2 ms in the clock is 0.002 x 299,792 km/s = 600 km of distance.BIPM, The International System of Units (SI Brochure), 9th edition, 2019 (definition of the metre); arithmetic. Wikipedia, Pseudorange: 'an error of one microsecond corresponds to an error of 300 metres'.
f4A receiver needs signals from at least four satellites: three for its position (x, y, z) and one more for its own clock error, which it solves for at the same time. With three spheres alone there are two possible points; one is normally far from the Earth's surface.Penn State GEOG 862, GPS and GNSS for Geospatial Professionals, 'The Navigation Solution' (e-education.psu.edu/geog862/node/1724); Wikipedia, Pseudorange; Kaplan and Hegarty, Understanding GPS/GNSS, 3rd ed., ch. 2.
f5The constellation has 32 operational satellites after the last GPS III satellite (SV-10) launched on 21 April 2026; it was 31 for several years. The US commits to at least 24 operational 95% of the time. The film says 'over thirty' and draws 32 on six orbital planes inclined 55 degrees.Lockheed Martin, GPS III (lockheedmartin.com/gps): 32 satellites in the operational constellation; CGSIC briefing of 21 April 2026 hosted on gps.gov; GPS.gov Space Segment (24-satellite commitment, six planes, 55 degrees). Read through search summaries, not opened in full; the count changes as satellites are launched and retired.
f6A GPS receiver is passive: it only receives the satellites' signals and sends nothing to them, which is why any number of users can use GPS at once. (A phone may download help data over its mobile network to start faster; that is not sent to the satellites.)Enge, 'The Global Positioning System: signals, measurements, and performance', Proc. IEEE 1999 ('a passive system... no transmissions from the users', unlimited concurrent users); Penn State GEOG 862, NAVSTAR GPS. Read through search summaries.
f7Each GPS satellite carries atomic clocks (rubidium; some older blocks also caesium; GPS III is rubidium only) and broadcasts the time each signal leaves, with its orbit data. The film shows a running time stamp as an illustration, not a real broadcast format.US Naval Observatory, Global Positioning System Overview (cnmoc.usff.navy.mil); Penn State GEOG 862, NAVSTAR GPS (courses.ems.psu.edu/geog862/node/1768); Kaplan and Hegarty, ch. 3. Read through search summaries.
f8The geometry is computed, not drawn by eye: you sit at 53 degrees from the equator's plane in the film's world, 18 degrees in front of the globe's edge; the four satellites are on the GPS orbit at azimuth/elevation (0, 90), (215, 25), (105, 25), (300, 45) as seen from you, at ranges 20,200, 23,244, 23,244 and 21,681 km. Each sphere's radius is that range. The circle of two spheres, and the two points of three (you, and one 5.6 Earth radii from the centre, far out in space), are solved by trilateration in storyboard.build.py. Satellites are drawn enlarged; distances are to scale.Arithmetic (storyboard.build.py, geometry.json).
f9With the phone's clock 2 ms fast every measured distance is 600 km short; solving three spheres at a time then gives four different points 600 to 1,600 km above you. Moving every sphere by the same amount (the clock correction) until all four meet brings them to one point: that is how a receiver finds its clock error. 2 ms is an example; real receivers solve whatever their error is.Arithmetic in storyboard.build.py from f3 and f4; the method: Penn State GEOG 862, 'The Navigation Solution'.
