How do noise-cancelling headphones erase sound?How do noise-cancelling headphones make a noise disappear?

PromiseThe headphones listen to the noise, make the same wave flipped upside down, and play it so the two add up to almost nothing. That works on steady low hums, whose waves are long and repeat; it fails on high or sudden sounds, so the cushions still block those.
AudienceCurious adults and teenagers anywhere, watching on a phone, English as a first or second language. Many own or have tried noise-cancelling headphones; they think the headphones just block sound, like earmuffs, and have not heard of anti-noise.

Fact sheet

IdClaimSource
f1Sound in air is a pressure wave: the air is pushed together (compressions, higher pressure) and pulled apart (rarefactions, lower pressure) in turn, and that pattern travels, at about 343 m/s in air at 20 °C. Each bit of air only rocks back and forth about where it is; the pattern is what travels.Standard acoustics (Kinsler, Frey et al., Fundamentals of Acoustics, 4th ed., ch. 5); speed of sound 331.3 x sqrt(1 + 20/273.15) = 343.2 m/s. From the textbook, not looked up in this session.
f2Active noise cancellation: a microphone picks up the incoming noise, the electronics make a signal of the opposite phase (the same wave upside down, 'anti-noise'), and the speaker plays it with your audio. Where the noise is high the anti-noise is low, so they add up to much less: destructive interference. The film's 'add up to almost nothing' is said of the steady hum it shows.Kuo and Morgan, 'Active noise control: a tutorial review', Proc. IEEE 87(6), 943-973, 1999; HowStuffWorks and BBC Science Focus explainers. Read through search summaries in the first cut; not reopened.
f3ANC works best on steady, low-frequency noise (engine hum, air conditioning) and less well on higher frequencies and on sudden or quickly changing sounds. NIOSH describes ANC as aimed at low-frequency noise, typically 500 Hz and below. No dB figure is stated in the film.CDC/NIOSH record stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/210761; Sennheiser support; Sonos; TechRadar. Read through search summaries.
f4Wavelength = speed / frequency. A 100 Hz hum: 343 / 100 = 3.43 m, said 'metres long', shown '≈ 3.4 m'. A 5,000 Hz squeak: 343 / 5,000 = 0.0686 m, said 'a few centimetres', shown '≈ 7 cm'. The two frequencies are examples chosen for the film.Arithmetic from f1.
f5The cups and cushions block sound passively, and passive isolation works better at mid and high frequencies than at low ones; so passive isolation handles the highs and ANC the lows. The film shows the squeak coming through weaker, as an illustration, without a figure.Sonos, 'How do noise-canceling headphones work'; Logitech, 'Passive noise isolation vs active noise cancellation'; Sennheiser support. Read through search summaries.
f6If a tone's flipped copy is late by d, what is left is 2 sin(pi f d) of the tone. With an example delay d = 1/30,000 s (0.033 ms): 100 Hz leaves 2.1% (shown 2%), 5,000 Hz leaves 100% (2 sin 30°). Both charts show the same 10 ms of time: 1 cycle of the hum, 50 of the squeak; the squeak's magnifier is marked ×20. 'Late' is drawn to the right on a time axis. The delay is an illustration, not a measured figure for any headphone.Arithmetic (sum of two equal sine waves with a phase difference of 2 pi f d).
f7Many headphones have a microphone inside the cup too, in front of the driver (feedback or 'hybrid' ANC). The model has both; the film explains the outside (feed-forward) one and names the inside one only in the model. The headphone is generic: no real product, no logo.Audio-Technica support, 'How do different active noise-cancelling headphones work'; SoundGuys, 'Noise canceling explained'; the 52Audio teardown of a 40 mm over-ear ANC headphone (feed-forward and feedback MEMS mics), all read through search results in this session.
f8The to-scale views put the head (about 15 cm across), the 100 Hz wave (3.43 m) and the 5,000 Hz wave (6.86 cm) in the same scene at 1 unit = 1 mm, with the 9.4 cm tall ear cup. Seen from above at 4.3 m with a 30 degree lens, the frame is about 2.3 m tall, so one 3.43 m wave runs past the top of the frame: the ruler is drawn on the part that shows and labelled with the full length.Arithmetic from f4; head breadth about 15 cm (typical adult); the cup's size is the model's (f9).
f9The headphone model: oval cups 76 x 94 mm, 30 mm deep; a 40 mm dynamic driver screwed to a baffle; feed-forward mic behind a grille on the outside of the cup; feedback MEMS mic in front of the driver; main board with the ANC chip behind the driver; a pouch battery; memory-foam cushions in a leather cover, 21 mm thick. The ear: pinna about 62 mm tall and 35 mm wide, canal about 25 mm long and 7 mm across, an eardrum about 9 mm across set at a slant.Teardowns of 40 mm over-ear ANC headphones (qucox.com teardown of a Technics EAH-A800; 52Audio teardowns of Sony WH-1000XM4 and WH-XB910N as summarised by The Walkman Blog; a Lumafield CT scan of over-ear headphones), read through search results in this session, not opened; ear canal 2.5 cm by 0.7 cm from the ear canal article in Wikipedia's medical mirror (search result); pinna and eardrum sizes from textbook anatomy, not looked up.
f10The clatter chart is an illustration: a sharp burst (a fast rise, then a ringing that dies away in a few milliseconds) and the chip's copy modelled as the burst smoothed (the chip cannot follow a change faster than about a millisecond) and late by the same 0.033 ms; what is left is computed as their sum. It is not a measurement.Arithmetic in film.js; the claim that ANC handles sudden sounds less well is f3.
f11The waves of light in the cup, in the air and over the chip are slowed down and not to scale (sound would cross the cup in a tenth of a millisecond); the to-scale views (f8) say so where they are.The film's own drawing convention, said on screen in s03.
