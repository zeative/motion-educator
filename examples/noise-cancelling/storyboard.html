How do noise-cancelling headphones erase sound?How do noise-cancelling headphones make a noise disappear?

PromiseThe headphones listen to the noise, make the same wave flipped upside down, and play it so the two add up to almost nothing. That works on steady low hums, whose waves are long and repeat; it fails on high or sudden sounds, so the cushions still block those.
AudienceCurious adults and teenagers anywhere, watching on a phone, English as a first or second language. Many own or have tried noise-cancelling headphones; they think the headphones just block sound, like earmuffs, and have not heard of anti-noise.

Fact sheet

IdClaimSource
f1Sound in air is a pressure wave: the air is pushed together (compressions, higher pressure) and pulled apart (rarefactions, lower pressure) in turn, and that pattern travels, at about 343 m/s in air at 20 °C. Each bit of air only rocks back and forth about where it is; the pattern is what travels.Standard acoustics (Kinsler, Frey et al., Fundamentals of Acoustics, 4th ed., ch. 5); speed of sound 331.3 x sqrt(1 + 20/273.15) = 343.2 m/s. From the textbook, not looked up in this session.
f2Active noise cancellation: a microphone picks up the incoming noise, the electronics make a signal of the opposite phase (the same wave upside down, 'anti-noise'), and the speaker plays it with your audio. Where the noise is high the anti-noise is low, so they add up to much less: destructive interference. The film's 'add up to almost nothing' is said of the steady hum it shows.Kuo and Morgan, 'Active noise control: a tutorial review', Proc. IEEE 87(6), 943-973, 1999 (abstract: anti-noise from secondary sources); HowStuffWorks, 'How noise-canceling headphones work'; BBC Science Focus, 'How do noise-cancelling headphones cancel sounds?'. Read through search summaries; the pages could not be opened from this session.
f3ANC works best on steady, low-frequency noise (engine hum, air conditioning) and less well on higher frequencies and on sudden or quickly changing sounds (voices, clatter). NIOSH describes ANC as aimed at low-frequency noise, typically 500 Hz and below. No dB figure is stated in the film: published figures vary widely by product and test (consumer sources give anything from 10 to 40 dB), so none is used.CDC/NIOSH record stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/210761 (ANC 'typically at 500 Hz and below'); Sennheiser support, 'What impacts ANC performance'; Sonos, 'How do noise-canceling headphones work'; TechRadar, 'What is active noise cancellation'. All read through search summaries; stacks.cdc.gov and the others could not be opened from this session.
f4Wavelength = speed / frequency. A 100 Hz hum: 343 / 100 = 3.43 m, said 'metres long', shown '≈ 3.4 m'. A 5,000 Hz squeak: 343 / 5,000 = 0.0686 m, said 'a few centimetres', shown '≈ 7 cm'. 100 Hz repeats 100 times a second. The two frequencies are examples chosen for the film, not measurements of a particular engine or squeak.Arithmetic from f1.
f5The cups and cushions block sound passively, and passive isolation works better at mid and high frequencies than at low ones; so passive isolation handles the highs and ANC the lows. The film shows the squeak coming through smaller, as an illustration, without a figure.Sonos, 'How do noise-canceling headphones work' (cushions passively block high frequencies; ANC the lowest); Logitech blog, 'Passive noise isolation vs active noise cancellation' (passive works on mid to high frequencies); Sennheiser support. Read through search summaries.
f6Why a delay matters more for a high sound: if a tone's flipped copy is late by a time d, what is left is 2 sin(pi f d) of the tone's size. With an example delay d = 1/30,000 s (0.033 ms): at 100 Hz, 2 sin(pi x 100 / 30,000) = 0.021, about 2% left; at 5,000 Hz, 2 sin(pi x 5,000 / 30,000) = 2 sin(30 degrees) = 1.00, nothing cancelled. The delay is an illustration, not a measured figure for any headphone; real headphones are designed so their cancelling fades out at high frequencies instead of adding noise there.Arithmetic (sum of two equal sine waves with a phase difference of 2 pi f d).
f7Simplification: many headphones also have a microphone inside the cup, near the ear (feedback or 'hybrid' ANC); the film shows only the outside (feedforward) microphone. The cut-through cup is a drawing, not to scale, and not any real product.BBC Science Focus and HowStuffWorks (feedforward and feedback designs), read through search summaries.
f8The to-scale view draws 400 px a metre: a 3.43 m wave is 1,372 px, a 6.86 cm wave 27.4 px, a head about 15 cm wide 62 px. Ten times closer (4,000 px a metre) the 6.86 cm wave is 274 px and an ear cup about 9 cm across is 360 px.Arithmetic from f4; head breadth about 15 cm (typical adult).
