Why do traffic jams appear out of nowhere?Why do traffic jams appear out of nowhere?

PromiseWith enough cars close together, one small slowdown grows from driver to driver into a jam that slides backwards while the cars go forwards; leaving a big gap and holding a steady speed soaks the wave up instead of passing it on.
AudienceAnyone who drives or rides in traffic, watching on X or Reddit, anywhere in the world. They have sat in a jam with no visible cause; they think jams need a crash, roadworks or a bottleneck.
Length79.37 s · 25 shots · 75 drawn frames · 1920×1080 · 60 fps · en · voice: Catherine

Night on a test track: the kit's graphite stage as the dark ground, the road a lit band of asphalt, the cars lit 3D models with glowing tail lights. Red (the accent) is braking and the jam, nothing else; cyan is the car that soaks the wave up, and the driver who started it. The stage light sits on the jam and follows it.

Fact sheet

IdClaimSource
f1In the experiment 22 cars drove on a single-lane circular road 230 m around, on flat ground with no bottleneck; they started almost evenly spaced at about 30 km/h, the drivers asked to cruise at about 30 km/h and to follow the car ahead safely. Published 4 March 2008. The gap between cars: 230 / 22 = 10.45 m front to front; with cars 4.5 m long (the model's assumption), about 6 m (5.95 m) bumper to bumper.Sugiyama, Fukui, Kikuchi, Hasebe, Nakayama, Nishinari, Tadaki and Yukawa, 'Traffic jams without bottlenecks: experimental evidence for the physical mechanism of the formation of a jam', New Journal of Physics 10 (2008) 033001 (read in full from the open-access copy, University of Osaka repository hdl.handle.net/11094/93262). The experiment was run at the track of Nakanihon Automotive College, Japan.
f2Small fluctuations grew until free flow broke down: the paper's snapshot 3 minutes after the start shows a jam, and several cars were forced to stop completely for a moment. The paper's point: a jam forms spontaneously once the density of cars passes a critical value; a bottleneck is only a trigger. The film's 'within minutes' and 'stopping dead' are these.Sugiyama et al. 2008 (f1), abstract, text and figure 3.
f3The jam cluster moved backwards at roughly 20 km/h while the cars kept going forwards (outside the jam at about 40 km/h); about 20 km/h is the common speed of jams measured on real highways (the paper compares it with aerial traces of highway traffic, Treiterer and Myers 1974).Sugiyama et al. 2008 (f1), text and figures 4 and 5.
f4The chain from one small slowdown to a stop is the film's model, not a measurement from the experiment: in sim.py one driver eases off from 30 to 25 km/h (-0.5 m/s2 for 3 s); as the slowdown passes back, the lowest speed of each driver it reaches is 24.6, 24.9, 24.8, 24.7, 24.5, 24.3, 24.0, 23.6, 23.1, 22.5, 21.8, 20.9, 19.9, 18.5, 16.5, 13.6, 8.7, 1.9, 0.0 km/h: the first few about the same, then each lower, until the 19th driver stops. Simplification said in words: 'the drivers behind react late and brake harder'.sim.py (this film), dips in sim.json.
f5The ring on screen is a computer model, labelled as one: 22 cars 4.5 m long on a 230 m ring, Intelligent Driver Model (Treiber, Hennecke and Helbing 2000) with a 0.35 s reaction delay, parameters set so that 30 km/h at this spacing is steady. Its jam moves -18.5 km/h (backwards), close to the experiment's 20; cars outside reach 50.3 km/h (the experiment: about 40); about 11.0 cars are stopped at a time (the experiment's jam held about 5). The model's first car stops 20 s after the slowdown; the real drivers took a few minutes, so the film never shows the model's clock, and its sped-up passages are tagged.sim.py and sim.json (this film); Treiber, Hennecke, Helbing, Phys. Rev. E 62, 1805 (2000).
f6In a later ring experiment (260 m, 21 to 22 cars, run by the University of Arizona, USA), one car whose speed was controlled by a computer (a human still steered) dampened the stop-and-go waves; one of its controllers simply estimated the average speed and drove at it, letting the gap ahead open when the car in front sped up and close when it slowed. 'One car with a computer on the pedals' is that car. The driving advice (a big gap, a steady speed, don't copy the brakes ahead) is what that controller does; the film does not claim a human study.Stern et al., 'Dissipation of stop-and-go waves via control of autonomous vehicles: Field experiments', Transportation Research Part C 89 (2018) 205-221 (read in full, arXiv:1705.01693), sections 2.1 and 3.2.
f7In the film's model, one car (car 11) switched to that controller 140 s in (holding the ring's measured average, 12.5 km/h, and a long gap) smooths the ring: the spread of speeds over a minute falls from 18.76 km/h to 0.0 km/h. The car ahead of it stops and starts; it and the cars behind it roll at an even speed.sim.py, scenario 'absorb' (this film).
f8'Enough cars, close together' is the critical density of f2; '≈ 6 m' on screen is the ring's 5.95 m bumper-to-bumper gap (f1).Sugiyama et al. 2008; arithmetic in f1.
