Why do onions make you cry?Why does cutting an onion make you cry?

PromiseAn onion's cells keep sulfur compounds and an enzyme apart; cutting bursts the cells, they mix, and a second enzyme turns the product into a gas that reaches your eyes and sets off nerves that make you tear up. A sharp knife bursts less and sprays far fewer droplets.
AudienceCurious adults and teenagers anywhere, watching on X or Reddit, mostly on a phone, often muted at first. They know onions sting the eyes and have heard tips, but not what happens inside the onion or the eye.

Fact sheet

IdClaimSource
f1Cutting an onion releases an irritating 'lachrymatory factor' that makes the eyes water; an intact onion does not release it, because the chemistry starts only when tissue is disrupted.Imai et al., 'An onion enzyme that makes the eyes water', Nature 419, 685 (2002), doi:10.1038/419685a (abstract read on nature.com); Kato et al., Scientific Reports 6, 23779 (2016): 'the LF is produced through the successive enzymatic reactions that occur upon onion tissue disruption' (read).
f2In the intact onion the sulfur-containing flavour precursors (S-alk(en)yl cysteine sulfoxides, mainly isoalliin) and the enzyme alliinase are kept in separate compartments; cutting breaks the cells and they meet. Simplification: the film draws the enzyme in the vacuole and the precursors in the cytoplasm around it, the classic finding (Lancaster & Collin 1981), but the exact compartments are still debated, and not every cell holds both (alliinase is concentrated in bundle-sheath cells). The board says 'simplified'.Lancaster & Collin, Plant Science Letters 22, 169 (1981), as summarised in a 2023 open-access paper (PMC10698848, read: 'clearly partitioned... the vacuoles exhibited alliinase activity while the alkyl cysteine sulfoxide precursor is present in the protoplasts'); Live Science, 'Why does slicing onions make you cry?' (2025, quoting J. Silvaroli, Ohio State; read).
f3Alliinase turns the sulfur compounds (isoalliin) into 1-propenesulfenic acid, which is unstable ('sulfenic acids are not very stable').Kato et al. 2016 (above, read); Wikipedia, syn-Propanethial-S-oxide (read); Live Science 2025 quoting Silvaroli (read).
f4The tear gas was presumed to form spontaneously from that acid after alliinase acted; Imai et al. (House Foods Corporation, Japan, with the Universities of Tokyo and Kyoto) showed in 2002 that it is made specifically by a previously unknown enzyme, lachrymatory-factor synthase. 'For decades': the gas was identified as propanethial S-oxide by Brodnitz and Pascale in 1971, 31 years before.Imai et al., Nature 419, 685 (2002), abstract and author affiliations (read on nature.com); Kato et al. 2016: LF 'identified as propanethial S-oxide almost 40 years ago by Brodnitz and Pascale' (1971).
f5The gas is syn-propanethial-S-oxide, (Z)-CH3CH2CH=S+-O-, formula C3H6OS, molar mass 90.14 g/mol: three carbons, six hydrogens, one sulfur, one oxygen; the sulfur is double-bonded to the third carbon and bonded to the oxygen.Wikipedia, syn-Propanethial-S-oxide (read): formula, structure, 'a volatile liquid that acts as a lachrymatory agent'.
f6It is volatile: it turns to vapour quickly, which is how it reaches the eyes. Simplification: the film draws it as rising wisps; it drifts on air currents. A 2025 study suggests tiny droplets thrown up by the cut carry it too (f8).Live Science 2025 quoting Silvaroli (read: 'a volatile liquid, meaning it turns to vapor very quickly... That's how it reaches your eyes and irritates sensory nerves'); Wikipedia (read).
f7On the eye it irritates the sensory nerves of the cornea; that signal reaches the brainstem (trigeminal nerve), which drives the tear (lacrimal) gland through parasympathetic nerves: reflex tears, which wash the irritant away. Simplification: the film draws one path in and one out.Wu et al. 2025 (below): 'activates the ciliary nerves in the cornea and cause tears'; Live Science 2025 quoting Silvaroli: 'Your eyes start tearing up to get rid of the irritant'; StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf) on the corneal and lacrimation reflexes (search summary only, not opened).
f8Blade sharpness: cutting with blades whose tip radius rose from 0.91 um (sharpest) to 13.3 um (bluntest) increased the number of droplets thrown up to forty-fold; blunter blades dent the soft tissue under the tough skin more before it breaks, so the juice comes out under pressure. Faster cuts threw about four times as many (0.44 to 2.03 m/s; not in the film). Chilling the onion is NOT in the film: the same team refrigerated onions (1 C, 12 h) and saw no difference in droplet speed and a noticeably larger volume of droplets, and no direct measurement that cold reduces the gas was found. 'Fewer tears' is the authors' own reading ('experimental validation for the widely held belief that sharpening knives reduces onion-induced tearing'); they measured droplets, not tears. The drawn tips are to scale with each other (13.3 / 0.91 = 14.6); the onion layer is not.Wu, Hooshanginejad, Wang, Hui & Jung (Cornell), 'Droplet outbursts from onion cutting', arXiv:2505.06016 (May 2025; read in full), published in PNAS 2025 (doi 10.1073/pnas.2512779122, per press coverage; the PNAS page could not be opened).
