IdClaimSource
f1Light travels at 299 792 458 m/s in vacuum (exact, by definition of the metre); in air it is 0,03% slower (refractive index about 1,0003), so about 299 700 km/s. The film rounds this to '≈ 300.000 km/detik' and uses the exact figure in the division.BIPM, The International System of Units (SI Brochure), 9th edition, 2019: definition of the metre. Refractive index of air: Ciddor 1996, Applied Optics 35, 1566.
f2Sound travels at about 343 m/s in dry air at 20 °C: 331,3 x sqrt(1 + 20/273,15) = 343,2 m/s. It depends on temperature (347 m/s at 27 °C, 349 m/s at 30 °C), so every distance in the film is marked '≈'. Simplification: the film uses the 20 °C figure everywhere; on a 30 °C evening the true distances are 1,7% larger.Standard formula for an ideal gas, e.g. US National Weather Service, Speed of Sound Calculator (weather.gov/epz/wxcalc_speedofsound); Kinsler et al., Fundamentals of Acoustics, 4th ed.
f3Light is about 874 000 times faster than sound in air: 299 792 458 / 343 = 874 030. Spoken as 'hampir sejuta kali' (nearly a million), written as '≈ 874.000×'.Arithmetic from f1 and f2.
f4Thunder is made by the lightning itself: the stroke heats the air in its channel to about 50 000 °F = (50 000 - 32) x 5/9 = 27 760 °C ≈ 28 000 °C within microseconds, the air expands explosively, and that shock becomes the sound. So the flash and the thunder start at the same moment and place. Simplification: thunder comes from the whole length of the channel, which is why it rumbles; the film draws the front as the channel's outline moving outward and times the arrival from the nearest point, the strike point.NOAA National Severe Storms Laboratory, Severe Weather 101: Lightning Basics and FAQ (nssl.noaa.gov/education/svrwx101/lightning); US National Weather Service, Understanding Lightning: Thunder (weather.gov/safety/lightning-science-thunder).
f5Over the 2 058 m of the first strike, light takes 2 058 / 299 792 458 = 0,0000069 s (shown as 0,000007 detik) and sound takes 2 058 / 343 = 6,00 s. The scene is drawn at 0,31 px a metre, so 2 058 m is 638 px and the front moves 343 x 0,31 = 106,3 px a second, in real time. The human figure is not to scale, and neither is the light in time: its shell is drawn crossing the scene in 0,15 s (4 200 px a second) because its true speed would be a millionth of a frame; the board gives the true figure.Arithmetic from f1 and f2.
f6Distance to a strike ≈ seconds between flash and thunder x 343 m. Three seconds: 3 x 343 = 1 029 m ≈ 1 km (one kilometre takes 1 000 / 343 = 2,9 s). The US weather service gives the same rule in miles: count the seconds and divide by five (1 609 m / 343 m/s = 4,7 s a mile).US National Weather Service, Understanding Lightning: Thunder (weather.gov/safety/lightning-science-thunder); arithmetic from f2.
