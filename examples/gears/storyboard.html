Why does a bigger rear gear make hills easier?Why does shifting to a bigger rear gear make a hill feel easier, when your legs are just as strong as before?

PromiseA small gear driving a big one trades speed for torque: with three times the teeth, the big gear turns three times slower but with three times the torque, and that is why a bigger rear gear makes a hill easier, at the price of more pedal turns.
AudienceCurious teenagers and adults anywhere who ride a bike and have shifted gears on a hill. They know gears from machines and clocks, and many have heard the word 'torque' from car or motorbike specs, but they have never seen why a small gear driving a big one turns it more strongly.

f1Meshing gears pass their teeth one for one, so the driven gear turns (teeth of the driver ÷ teeth of the driven) times for each turn of the driver; two external gears turn in opposite directions. The film's s04 numbers three teeth as they pass and s05 turns the gears opposite ways.Bureau of Naval Personnel, Basic Machines (NAVPERS 10624, 1946), ch. 6 'Gears', pp. 71-73: 'Wheel A has 10 teeth which mesh with the 40 teeth on wheel B. Wheel A will have to rotate four times to cause B to make one revolution'; 'When two external gears mesh, they rotate in opposite directions' (archive.org/details/BasicMachines10624). Also Shigley & Mischke, Mechanical Engineering Design, ch. 13 (via Wikipedia, 'Gear train').
f2The film's pair: 12 teeth driving 36. Ratio 36 ÷ 12 = 3. One turn of the 12-tooth gear moves 12 teeth past the mesh, which is 12/36 = 1/3 of a turn of the big gear = 120°. On screen: '12', '36', '1 TURN · 360°', '1/3 TURN · 120°', 'a third'.Arithmetic from f1 (storyboard.build.py asserts it).
f3At the pitch point the tooth of each gear pushes the other with the same force F (action and reaction). Torque is force times the distance from the axle, so the small gear's torque is F × 18 mm and the big gear's F × 54 mm: three times as much. This is the gear's mechanical advantage, teeth of the driven ÷ teeth of the driver = 36/12 = 3 (ideal, with no losses). On screen: 'r 18', 'r 54', '54 ÷ 18 = 3', 'TORQUE ×3'. Simplification: the tooth force really acts along the line of action at the 20° pressure angle; the arrows show its tangential part, which is the part that turns the gear; friction losses (small for spur gears) are left out.Basic Machines (1946), ch. 6, 'Magnifying force with gears', p. 74: 'M.A. (theoretical) = To/Ta, in which To = number of teeth on driven gear; Ta = number of teeth on driver gear', and 'wherever there is a speed reduction, the effect of the effort you apply is multiplied'; Wikipedia, 'Gear train', 'Torque ratio analysis using virtual work' and 'Mechanical advantage' (MA = N_B / N_A).
f4The pair's dimensions, standard spur gears of module m = 3 mm: pitch diameter d = z × m = 36 mm (12 teeth) and 108 mm (36 teeth), so pitch radii 18 and 54 mm; tip diameter d + 2m (42 and 114 mm); root diameter d − 2.5m (28.5 and 100.5 mm); centre distance (12 + 36) × 3 / 2 = 72 mm. Drawn at 6 px per mm. On screen: 'Ø108 · 36 TEETH', 'Ø36 · 12 TEETH', 'm 3'. Simplification: the teeth are drawn as straight-flanked trapezoids at true addendum and dedendum, not exact involutes, and a real 12-tooth 20° gear is usually profile-shifted to avoid undercut (KHK's own 12-tooth example uses x = +0.6); the drawing shows the unshifted standard proportions.KHK Gears, Gear Technical Reference, 'Calculation of Gear Dimensions', 4.1 Spur gears, Table 4.1: reference diameter d = zm, addendum 1.00m, tooth depth 2.25m, tip diameter d + 2m, root diameter d − 2.5m (khkgears.net).
f5A gear pair exchanges speed for torque: what the driven gear gains in torque it loses in speed (1/3 the speed × 3 times the torque = the same power, minus friction). On screen: 'Speed is traded for torque', 'TURNS 1/3', 'TORQUE ×3', 'TRADED'.University of Florida, EML2322L Design and Manufacturing Lab, 'Gears and gearing': 'The primary purpose of gears is to exchange speed for torque or vice-versa' (web.mae.ufl.edu/designlab); Wikipedia, 'Gear train' (virtual work: torque ratio = speed ratio).

f9With the same chainring and the same push on the pedals, the chain's pull is the same whichever rear sprocket it is on (pull = crank torque ÷ chainring pitch radius). The rear sprocket is fixed to the wheel while pedalling, so the torque at the wheel is that pull × the sprocket's pitch radius: 66.80 / 22.54 = 2.96 times as much on the 33 as on the 11; averaged over a turn it is exactly 33/11 = 3 (the work done per pedal turn is the same). On screen: 'The chain pulls the same', 'The 33 gives the wheel 3 times the torque', 'TORQUE ×3'. Losses in the chain are left out.Basic Machines (1946), ch. 3 'The wheel and axle', p. 33: 'When you are pedaling, the sprocket is fixed to the wheel, so the combination is a true wheel-and-axle machine'; torque = force × radius and MA = teeth ratio as in f3; Wikipedia, 'Gear train' ('The speed ratios of chain and belt drives are computed in the same way as gear ratios'). Arithmetic in storyboard.build.py.
f10Distance per pedal turn: the chain moves 32 teeth per crank turn, so the wheel turns 32/11 = 2.91 times on the 11 and 32/33 = 0.97 times on the 33. The tyre's circumference is π × 736.3 = 2313 mm, so one pedal turn moves the bike 6.73 m on the 11 and 2.24 m on the 33: three times the pedal turns for the same distance. On screen: '6.7 m', '2.2 m', '1 PEDAL TURN'.Arithmetic from f7 and f8 (tyre outer diameter 736.3 mm from bikeinsights.com), shown in storyboard.build.py; rolling without slip.
f11Why the hill feels lighter: climbing needs a certain force at the tyre; on the bigger sprocket the same push on the pedal gives about three times the turning force at the wheel (f9), so a third of the push does the same job, at a third of the distance per pedal turn (f10). On screen: 'A bigger rear gear makes a hill feel easy', 'Slower, but much easier', 'SAME PUSH'.Follows from f9 and f10; the trade of speed for torque is f5 (UF EML2322L).
